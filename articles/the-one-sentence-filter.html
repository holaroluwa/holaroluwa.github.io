Why long lists feel like progress

For a long time I confused having a long list with making progress.

A list of 104 companies to email. A to-do list so full it had stopped telling me anything. It all felt like readiness. It was mostly just weight.

The thing that changed it is small, and you can do it in the next ten minutes on whatever list is sitting heaviest on you right now. I use AI for the sorting, but the method is the point, not the tool.

Step 1: Write one sentence that defines a fit

One sentence. Specific enough that you can pick up any item on the list, hold it against the sentence, and get a clean yes or no.

For my prospect list it was:

"A B2B company with a sales team of 5 to 30 people that is hiring at least one SDR right now."

Every phrase in it is a filter. "Sales team of 5 to 30" rules out both solo founders and enterprise. "Hiring an SDR right now" means the problem I solve is live for them today.

If you sit down to write this sentence and you can't, that's the real finding. It means you've been working a list without knowing what you're looking for, and no amount of AI cleans that up. Write the sentence first.

Step 2: Ask the tool to score, not to choose

Open ChatGPT or Claude. Paste the list and the sentence. Then use a prompt close to this one:

Here is a list. A good fit looks like this: [your one sentence]. Score every item from 1 to 5 for how well it matches that sentence. For anything you score 3 or lower, give me a one-line reason. Return it as a table, sorted best to worst.

Ask for the score and the reasons, not "which ones are best." The difference matters. A ranked list with reasons lets you see how the tool read your sentence, so you can catch it when it gets you wrong.

Step 3: Read the reasons before you cut anything

This is the step everyone skips, and it's where the value is.

The reasons on the low scores do two jobs. They tell you what to remove, and they tell you where your sentence was too loose. When I ran mine, a batch of companies scored 2 with the reason "no sign of a sales team." I had written "sales team of 5 to 30" but half my list was companies I hoped had one. The sentence was a wish, not a filter. I tightened it and ran it again.

Step 4: Work the top, and trust a short list

Take the 4s and 5s and start there. If that pile looks small, that's the method working, not failing. A short list you actually believe in beats a long one you're avoiding.

My 104 companies became about 40. I stopped feeling behind on the other 64, because they were never really the list.

This isn't just a sales trick

I learned it on a prospect list, but I now run it on almost everything that piles up.

A job hunt

"A role where I'd be the second or third GTM hire, at a company past its seed round." Paste the postings, score them, apply to the 4s.

A feature backlog

"Something a paying user has asked for twice in the last month." Score the backlog, build from the top.

A week's tasks, Sunday night

"Moves a real number or a real conversation forward by Friday." Score the list, do those first.

The one idea underneath

The AI can rank a hundred things against a clear standard in seconds. It cannot decide what your standard is. That decision, compressed into one honest sentence, is the entire skill.

Most people never write the sentence. They keep the long list instead, because a long list feels like being prepared, and writing the sentence feels like admitting most of it doesn't count.

Write the sentence. Let the tool sort. Work the top.

Do I need a paid AI tool for this?

No. A free ChatGPT or Claude account runs this exact prompt. The method doesn't need a paid plan or a specific model.

What if I genuinely can't write the one sentence?

That's the real finding, not a failure of the method. It means you've been working the list without knowing what you're actually filtering for. Sit with that before you touch the AI part.

Why score instead of just asking AI to pick the best ones?

A bare pick hides the model's reasoning. A score with a reason attached lets you check its work and catch it misreading your sentence, which happens more than you'd expect.

Does this only work for sales prospect lists?

No. The same shape applies to a job hunt, a product feature backlog, or a plain Sunday-night task list. Anything that piled up past the point of being useful.
